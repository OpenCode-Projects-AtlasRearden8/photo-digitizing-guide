Glossary

Every term used in this guide, defined once, in plain English.

300 dpi / 600 dpi — Scan density settings. Dots (pixels) captured per inch of original. See chapter 3 for when each is honest.

3-2-1 rule — Keep 3 copies of important data, on 2 types of storage, with 1 copy off-site. Simplified for households in chapter 6.

35mm — The standard film format for consumer slides and negatives; each frame is about 24×36 mm.

Archive (verb) — To place files into the permanent, organized structure (masters/) where they are backed up and never edited.

Backlight — Light shining through film from behind. Film is transparent and must be backlit to be digitized; prints are front-lit.

Batch — A group of photos captured, named, and filed in one sitting. The unit of work in the worksheets.

Checksum — A digital fingerprint of a file. If two copies of a file have the same checksum, they are byte-for-byte identical — stronger proof than "it opened."

Decade folder — The first level inside masters/ (1950s/, 1960s/…). Broad enough that filing is never ambiguous.

DPI (dots per inch) — Sampling density of a scan. Higher dpi = more pixels per inch of the original, up to the point where the original has no more detail to give.

Flatbed scanner — A scanner with a glass bed you lay photos on. Even lighting, no contact with the photo, and (with a transparency adapter) film capability.

Glare / hot spot — A white reflection blob on a photo caused by light hitting it straight on. Eliminated by side lighting at ~45°.

Interpolation — A scanner or software inventing pixels to reach a claimed resolution. The reason "2400 dpi" from a cheap device may contain no more real detail than 600.

JPEG — A compressed, lossy image format. Universal and small; used for sharing copies, never for masters.

Keystone distortion — The trapezoid shape a photo gets when photographed at a slight angle instead of straight-on.

Light pad — A thin, evenly lit panel (made for tracing) used to backlight film for phone capture.

Masters — The untouched, best-quality scan files in masters/. The single source of truth; every share and print derives from them.

Medium format film — Larger film (120 roll film, ~6 cm frames) from older cameras; holds more detail than 35mm and rewards higher scan resolution.

Metadata — Information about a photo (date, people, place). In this guide it lives in filenames and notes.txt files, so it needs no special software.

Negative — Film with tones reversed (dark looks light). Scanned and digitally inverted to a positive image.

Off-site copy — A backup stored in a different building than the originals. Survives fire, flood, and theft.

Photos per hour — The realistic throughput measure: ~150–200/hour careful phone capture of prints; ~20–40/hour flatbed.

Print service — A company (online or a store kiosk) that prints your digital files onto photo paper.

Resolution — How much detail an image contains, in pixels. Discussed honestly in chapter 3.

Slide (transparency) — Film mounted in a small cardboard/plastic frame, viewed by light shining through. Backlit scanning required.

Sort order — The order files appear when listed alphabetically. Zero-padded names and ISO dates keep it equal to chronological order.

TIFF — An uncompressed, lossless image format. The correct format for master scans; large files.

Transparency adapter — A flatbed scanner's built-in backlight in the lid, which allows scanning slides and negatives.

Verification — Actually opening files from a backup to prove the backup works. An unverified backup is a hope, not a backup.

Zero-padding — Writing sequence numbers as 001, 002… 010 so alphabetical sort matches numeric sort.
