Lighting Setups

Good lighting is the difference between a phone capture that looks like a scan and one that looks like a snapshot of a snapshot. This chapter gives you three setups, from free to twenty dollars, that produce even, glare-free light.

The problem lighting solves

A photo print has a slightly glossy surface. Point any light straight at it and you get a hot spot — a white glare blob that erases part of the image forever. The fix is always the same principle: light the photo from the sides, at an angle, with light that is even across the whole surface.

Setup 1: Window daylight (free)

The cheapest setup is also one of the best.

  1. Pick a bright overcast day (direct sun creates hard shadows and shifts color).
  2. Find a window with indirect light.
  3. Lay a white or light-gray sheet on the floor or table near the window.
  4. Place photos on the sheet.
  5. Stand directly over the photo and shoot straight down.

Why it works: a large window is a huge, diffuse light source — the same idea as a professional softbox. Light arrives from a broad angle, so no single direction produces glare.

Watch for: your own shadow. Shift position until it falls away from the photo.

Setup 2: Two lamps at 45° (near free)

For evening work or windowless rooms:

  1. Two identical lamps (daylight-white bulbs, 4000–5000 K, if you can choose).
  2. Place them left and right of the photo, each about 45 degrees from the photo's surface and 30–45 cm (12–18 in) away.
  3. The photo sits on a matte white or neutral gray surface.

Why it works: light from two opposing 45° angles cancels glare — the reflection from one lamp lands where the other's doesn't. This is the classic copy-stand lighting arrangement, unchanged for 70 years.

Watch for: mismatched bulbs. Two different color temperatures will tint half your photo warm and half cool. Use identical bulbs.

Setup 3: The folding jig (about $15–25)

For sessions over an hour, build a repeatable jig:

  • A foldable LED photography light panel or two cheap LED panels on small stands.
  • A phone tripod or overhead mount pointed straight down.
  • A matte white board as the work surface.

The payoff is consistency: once set, every capture uses identical light, distance, and angle. Batch after batch looks the same, which makes chapter 4's file naming and any later editing far easier.

Rules that apply to every setup

  1. Turn off the flash. Always. A flash from a phone is the fastest way to create a glare blob.
  2. Turn off other room lights that mix color temperatures with your main light.
  3. Wipe the photo and the lens. A microfiber cloth on both; dust reads as a scan defect.
  4. Lock exposure and focus on the photo itself (tap and hold on most phone cameras), so it doesn't re-meter between shots.
  5. Check each capture at full zoom before moving the photo. Glare and blur are obvious at 100%; invisible on the phone's small preview.
  6. Photograph in batches by size — all the 4×6s, then all the 3×5s — so your framing and distance stay constant.

Lighting for film (slides and negatives)

Film is transparent: it must be backlit, not front-lit. Your options:

  • Flatbed with transparency adapter — the default choice (see chapter 1).
  • A small light pad (the kind used for tracing, $15–30) plus your phone on a tripod. Lay the negative strip or slides on the pad, shoot straight down with the room lights off. Keep the pad on its lowest brightness and expose so the sprocket holes/film edges look near-white, not blazing.

Never backlight film with a phone screen or a bare lamp — uneven light and visible texture will ruin the capture.

Quick self-check

Before a long session, do one test capture of each photo type and check:

  • No glare anywhere on the image
  • All four corners in focus
  • The photo's edges are parallel to the frame's edges
  • Colors look neutral (a white area of the photo should read white, not orange or blue)

If all four pass, your lighting is done. Move on to chapter 3.
