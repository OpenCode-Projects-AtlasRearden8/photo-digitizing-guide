# Resolution Honesty: 600 dpi vs. 300 dpi

Scan resolution marketing is a swamp of big numbers. This chapter tells you what dpi actually measures, what each common setting really delivers, and which one to use — with no overselling.

## What dpi actually is

**DPI = dots per inch.** A 600 dpi scan samples 600 pixels across every inch of the original. A 4×6 print scanned at 600 dpi becomes a 2400 × 3600 pixel file. At 300 dpi, the same print becomes 1200 × 1800 pixels.

The key insight: **the dpi you need depends on the size you'll ever want to view or print the image at, and on how much detail the original contains.** Bigger numbers are not automatically better — beyond the original's actual detail, extra dpi just makes a bigger file of the same picture.

## The honest table

| Original | 300 dpi gives you | 600 dpi gives you | Honest verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3×5 print | 1500 × 1050 px (good 3×5 reprint) | 3000 × 2100 px (good 6×10 reprint) | 300 is fine for reprinting same-size; 600 buys enlargement headroom |
| 4×6 print | 1800 × 1200 px | 3600 × 2400 px | 300 is fine for same-size; 600 if it's a favorite worth reprinting larger |
| 5×7 print | 2100 × 1500 px | 4200 × 3000 px | 300 is plenty for nearly everyone |
| 8×10 print | 2400 × 3000 px | 4800 × 6000 px | 300 is plenty; 600 files get huge |
| 35mm slide | ~1050 × 1500 px | ~2100 × 3000 px | **600 minimum.** Slides are tiny; you're enlarging 35mm frames to print size, so more dpi is genuinely useful |
| 35mm negative | same as slide | same as slide | **600 minimum**, same reason |

## The three honest rules

### Rule 1: For ordinary prints, 300 dpi is honest quality

A standard photo print holds roughly 200–300 real lines of detail per inch. Scanning at 300 dpi captures essentially all of it. Claiming a 4×6 print "needs" 1200 dpi is marketing: the extra data is interpolation or empty magnification.

**Use 300 dpi when:** the original is 5×7 or larger, or you only ever expect to view on screens and reprint at the same size.

### Rule 2: For small originals you may enlarge, 600 dpi is honest quality

Small originals (3×5 and smaller) and all film benefit from 600 dpi because the *enlargement factor* is higher. A 35mm slide is 24×36 mm — to make a 4×6 print from it, the scanner must stretch every film millimeter across half an inch of paper. More sampling at the scan stage is real information here, not padding.

**Use 600 dpi when:** originals are small prints you might reprint larger, all slides, all negative strips, and any photo you'd call a family treasure.

### Rule 3: Above 600 dpi, returns collapse

Scanner makers advertise 1200, 2400, even 6400 dpi. For reflective prints, beyond 600 dpi you are mostly scanning the texture of the paper's fibers. For 35mm film, 2400–3200 dpi captures nearly everything the film itself recorded; beyond that you hit the film's own grain structure. These settings multiply file size and scan time while adding detail you can't see at normal viewing.

**Use higher than 600 dpi only when:** you have a specific large-print reproduction project from small film, and you've tested that your scanner's optics (not interpolation) actually deliver it.

## File size reality check

Approximate uncompressed sizes for a 4×6 print:

- 300 dpi TIFF: ~6 MB
- 600 dpi TIFF: ~25 MB
- 1200 dpi TIFF: ~100 MB

A 200-photo shoebox at 600 dpi TIFF is roughly **5 GB**. At 300 dpi it's 1.2 GB. Both are manageable — but only if you planned for it (chapter 6). Scanning everything at 1200 dpi "just in case" turns a weekend project into a hard-drive problem for no visible benefit.

## Format: TIFF for masters, JPEG for sharing

- **Scan to TIFF** (lossless) for your master archive. Every future edit starts from the best file you have.
- **Export JPEG** (quality 85–90) copies for sharing with relatives and for the keepsake book in chapter 8.

Keep the two apart in your folder tree (chapter 5) so a shared JPEG never replaces the master TIFF.

## The one-line honesty test

> If you can't name the print size you'd need the extra resolution for, you don't need the extra resolution.
