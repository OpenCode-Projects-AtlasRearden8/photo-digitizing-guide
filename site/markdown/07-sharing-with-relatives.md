# Sharing with Relatives

An archive that only you can see is half-finished. This chapter covers getting copies to siblings, cousins, and elders — with and without the cloud — plus the etiquette that keeps it pleasant.

## First: share copies, never masters

Chapter 5 built a `shares/` tree for exactly this. The routine:

1. Copy the relevant photos from `masters/` into a new folder under `shares/`.
2. Convert or export them as **JPEG, quality 85–90** (chapter 3's format rule).
3. Optionally resize to a long edge of 3000–4000 px — plenty for viewing and ordinary prints, a quarter of the file size of a 600 dpi TIFF.
4. Name the folder for the recipient and occasion: `2026-03_family-album-for-aunt-ruth/`.

Everything in `shares/` is disposable — delete it when the sharing is done and rebuild it any time from masters.

## Sharing without any cloud account

### USB drive by hand (the classic)

Copy the `shares/` folder to a $10 flash drive and hand it over at the next visit. Include a plain-text `READ-ME-FIRST.txt`:

```
These are scans of the family photos, Christmas set 1974-1990.
To view: double-click any file.
To keep: copy the whole folder to your computer.
From: (your name), March 2026.
```

This works for every relative regardless of technical comfort, and it's how the off-site backup drive (chapter 6) can double as a delivery — drop off copies *and* store the drive in one trip.

### Direct file transfer between phones/computers

- **Nearby sharing built into the OS** (AirDrop, Quick Share, Nearby Share): instant for a handful of photos at a family gathering.
- **A USB cable between phone and computer:** still the most reliable transfer that exists.
- **A home network folder:** if your household already has one, it's private and free. If you don't, don't build one just for this.

### Email and chat apps, honestly

Fine for 5–10 photos, painful beyond that: attachments compress, chat apps compress harder, and a 40-photo email thread is nobody's archive. Use them for previews ("look what I found"), then deliver the real set on a drive.

## Sharing with a cloud service — the honest caveats

Shared-album features (photo-platform links, file-locker folders) are convenient for far-away relatives and there's no need to moralize about them. Know the trade-offs and decide:

- Someone else's terms of service sit between your family and the photos.
- Links expire; accounts get abandoned; free tiers compress or quota.
- The archive still lives in your two backed-up places — **the cloud copy is a convenience, never a pillar.**

If you use one, upload from `shares/`, never from `masters/`.

## Etiquette that matters in family photo projects

1. **Ask before publishing.** Sharing with the family and posting publicly are different acts. Faces of children, in particular: ask the parents.
2. **Name people with their permission.** Some relatives do not want their name attached to a searchable filename.
3. **Give elders the prints.** A 90-year-old aunt may not want a zip file. Chapter 8's keepsake prints are the better gift — often the most appreciated outcome of the whole project.
4. **Accept corrections gracefully.** "That's not 1974, that's 1972 — and that's Carol, not Karen." Every correction is free metadata. Write it into the event's `notes.txt` immediately.
5. **Set expectations for scope.** "I'm doing the 1960s this spring" prevents the whole family assigning you the entire attic.

## A simple release rhythm

Families sustain projects better than individuals do:

- **After each decade:** send a short note ("1960s folder is done — 214 photos, drive coming your way").
- **Once a year:** refresh the off-site drive (chapter 6) and hand a relative the newest complete copy. Your backup and your sharing now reinforce each other.
