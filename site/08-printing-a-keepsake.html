Printing a Keepsake

Digitizing is rescue; a keepsake is the reward. This chapter covers turning selected scans back into physical objects — a photo book, framed prints, or a small album — using the archive you built in chapters 1–7.

Start from masters, decide from shares

  1. Browse your masters/ tree (or a fresh export of it) and shortlist 30–100 favorites. Don't over-select: a book of 60 well-chosen photos gets looked at; 400 gets shelved.
  2. Copy shortlisted files into a projects/2026-XX_keepsake-book/ folder (chapter 5's tree).
  3. From there, work only on copies. Masters stay untouched.

Resolution honesty for printing (chapter 3, applied)

The rule from chapter 3 runs in reverse for printing:

  • A print needs roughly 300 pixels per inch at the printed size.
  • Your 4×6 print scanned at 300 dpi (1800 × 1200 px) prints beautifully at 4×6 and acceptably at 5×7.
  • The same file stretched to 8×10 will look soft. Don't do it — choose a different photo or a smaller print size.
  • A 35mm slide scanned at 600 dpi (~2100 × 3000 px) prints sharply at 8×10 — this is why film got the 600 dpi treatment.

The quick check: divide the file's pixel width by your target print width in inches. 300+ = sharp. 200–300 = acceptable at viewing distance. Below 200 = reconsider.

Keepsake options, cheapest first

1. Loose prints from a print service ($0.10–0.40 each)

Upload JPEGs from projects/ to a photo-print service (or use a local drugstore/grocery kiosk) and receive standard prints. Perfect for: handing sets to relatives, a "brag book" for an elder, test prints before committing to a book.

Order one test print first. Check color and sharpness in person before ordering 60.

2. A simple photo album and printed photos ($20–40)

Classic slip-in albums are underrated: no software, no design decisions, done in an evening. Sort prints chronologically — your years-and-events filenames (chapter 4) make this trivial — and write dates and names on the memo lines.

3. A printed photo book ($30–80)

Book services let you lay out pages in the browser. Honest advice:

  • Keep layouts simple: one photo per page for favorites, clean white backgrounds.
  • Put the story in captions — pull from your notes.txt files. "Ellen and Gramma, reunion, July 1987" is worth more than any design flourish.
  • 8×10" or 10×10" books show old photos well; pocket-sized books get handled more.
  • Order one copy first, proof it in person, then order copies for relatives.

4. Framed prints ($10–30 each)

One great scan, matted and framed, beats ten gadgets. Print at the sharpness-checked size, use a mat so the print doesn't touch the glass, and keep it out of direct sunlight — the same light that glare-fought you in chapter 2 will fade the print.

Paper and finish, briefly

  • Luster or satin finish handles fingerprints and glare better than glossy; matte flatters old, soft photos.
  • For anything you want to last generations, look for "archival" or "acid-free" paper claims on the print service — and remember your masters are the real long-term preservation; any print is a view of them.

Captions and dates

A keepsake without names is a mystery for the next generation. Before finalizing any book or album:

  • Fill in dates from filenames (zeros in a date mean "unknown" — write "1974, spring" or just "1974").
  • Confirm names with an elder relative before printing. Chapter 7's correction rule applies double here: a printed book cements errors.
  • Include a title page or first page: "The Asdale Family Photos, 1950–1990, collected 2026." Future-you's descendants will thank you.

The loop closes

Print → gift → relatives correct your dates → you fix notes.txt and filenames → the archive improves. That loop — digital informing paper, paper informing digital — is the whole point of the project.
